# Consulting Presentation Playbook

This reference supports the `presentation-script` skill.

## 1. The governing thought

A strong presentation is not a collection of slides. It is an argument.

Before scripting:
1. Identify the question being answered.
2. Write the answer in one sentence.
3. Check that each slide either:
   - establishes context,
   - supports the answer,
   - addresses a counterpoint,
   - explains implementation,
   - or enables a decision.

If a slide does none of these, treat it as secondary.

## 2. Pyramid communication

Default hierarchy:

**Answer**
- Reason 1
  - Evidence
- Reason 2
  - Evidence
- Reason 3
  - Evidence

The spoken version should normally reveal the answer before the supporting detail.

Weak:
> “We reviewed five processes, interviewed the teams, assessed turnaround time, and looked at error rates…”

Strong:
> “The current process is creating avoidable delay in three places. We found that through process timing, error analysis, and team interviews.”

## 3. The 1–3–1 pattern

For complex slides:

**1 message → up to 3 supporting points → 1 conclusion**

This helps the audience retain the structure.

Example:
> “There are three reasons we recommend Option B: it provides the best risk coverage, it is simpler to implement, and it preserves future flexibility. Taken together, those advantages outweigh its slightly higher upfront cost.”

## 4. Translate evidence into meaning

For every important number ask:

- Compared with what?
- Is it large or small?
- Is it increasing or decreasing?
- Where is it concentrated?
- Is it expected or surprising?
- Does it change a decision?

Do not leave numbers uninterpreted.

## 5. Executive compression

An executive usually needs:
- what happened;
- why it matters;
- what you recommend;
- what you need from them.

A technical audience may also need:
- method;
- assumptions;
- limitations;
- validation.

## 6. Consultant phrasing patterns

### Opening a slide
- “The key message is…”
- “This analysis points to…”
- “There are two things to take away.”
- “The most important finding here is…”

### Explaining contrast
- “The important difference is…”
- “What changes between the two options is…”
- “The headline looks similar, but the underlying drivers are different.”

### Explaining causality carefully
- “The pattern is consistent with…”
- “This suggests…”
- “The slide shows association, not causation.”
- “We would need additional analysis to confirm the driver.”

### Bringing out the so-what
- “What this means in practice is…”
- “The implication for the program is…”
- “This changes the decision in one important way…”
- “The risk is not the average; it is the concentration.”

### Recommending
- “We recommend…”
- “Our recommendation is to…”
- “Given these trade-offs, we would prioritize…”
- “The practical next step is…”

### Transitioning
- “That establishes the problem. The next slide looks at the root cause.”
- “With the opportunity sized, the next question is where to focus.”
- “We now know what needs to change; the next section explains how.”
- “That is the case for change. Let me turn to implementation.”

## 7. Memorable communication

Use memory aids selectively.

### Contrast
> “More alerts do not necessarily mean more coverage.”

### Compression
> “Clarity before activity.”

### Analogy
> “A monitoring model is like a net: making it tighter catches more, but it can also fill with noise.”

Always reconnect the analogy to the actual analysis.

### Concrete example
Turn abstract statements into something observable.

Abstract:
> “The process is inefficient.”

Concrete:
> “Analysts download three reports, reconcile them manually, and rebuild the same management pack each week.”

## 8. Storytelling

Use stories when they help establish meaning, not as decoration.

A compact story:
- Situation
- Tension
- Action
- Resolution
- Lesson

For leadership presentations, authenticity and specific details are more persuasive than theatrical language.

## 9. Delivery

### Pace
Default conversational professional pace:
- 125–150 words/minute.

Slow down for:
- recommendation;
- important number;
- key contrast;
- decision ask.

### Pause
A one-beat pause is useful:
- after the slide's core message;
- before the recommendation;
- after an important number;
- before moving to the next section.

### Emphasis
Emphasize only a few words per slide.

Example:
> “The issue is not **volume**. It is **relevance**.”

## 10. Common failure modes

### Reading the slide
Fix: interpret and prioritize.

### Too many points
Fix: cap at three.

### No “so what”
Fix: explicitly state implication.

### Unsupported overreach
Fix: distinguish observed fact from interpretation.

### Generic transitions
Weak:
> “Moving on to the next slide…”

Strong:
> “That tells us where the problem sits; now we can look at what is driving it.”

### Consultant jargon
Weak:
> “We need to operationalize a holistic transformation journey.”

Strong:
> “We need to change the process, clarify ownership, and automate the recurring steps.”

## 11. Presentation QA rubric

Score each slide 1–5:

| Dimension | 1 | 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Answer-first | message buried | message immediate |
| Grounding | unsupported claims | fully slide-supported |
| Structure | rambling | clear 1–3–1 |
| So-what | absent | explicit implication |
| Spoken quality | essay-like | natural speech |
| Audience fit | wrong level | tailored |
| Memorability | generic | concise anchor |
| Transition | disconnected | advances story |

Target: no dimension below 4 for key slides.
